# Second-order thinking

**Job.** Ask what happens next, including how other people respond, after the obvious first effect.

**Not this.** The question is whether your expectation differs from a consensus already reflected in a price. That is second-level thinking. A full stock-and-flow model is for when you need magnitudes and delays quantified.

**Diagram.** A chain: action, first-order effect, who responds, second-order effect, and where it crosses back into the original problem.

**Steps.**
1. State the action and its intended first-order effect.
2. Name who else adapts because of that effect: customers, competitors, colleagues, the system that was measured.
3. For each adapter, state what they do next, not what you wish they did.
4. Follow one more step where that response changes the original stock, incentive, or story.
5. Keep the action, change it, or drop it based on the later effect. "And then what?" asked once is not the tool. Asked until the chain closes or dies is the tool.

**Source.** No single originator. The consequence chain is the popular "and then what?" question. Farnam Street's article is an index, and it equates that question with Howard Marks's second-level thinking. This page refuses that equation. Marks stays on the second-level page. The named adapters, the return into the original stock or incentive, and asking until the chain closes or dies are this page's steps.
