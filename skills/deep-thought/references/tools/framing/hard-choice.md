# Hard Choice Model

**Job.** Tell what kind of choice this is before anyone scores the options.

**Not this.** High impact and easy to compare. Score that with a decision matrix. Missing information is not a hard choice. Name the fact and go get it. Do not use this page to avoid a choice that is merely unpleasant.

**Diagram.** Impact (high or low) by comparability (easy or hard). One cell marked. On the hard-choice cell, a note if the options are on a par.

**Steps.**
1. List the options and what each commits you to keep, and to give up.
2. Ask Wes O'Haire's two questions: how large is the impact, and how easy is it to compare the options?
3. Low impact, easy to compare: no-brainer. Pick and move on.
4. Low impact, hard to compare: apples and oranges. Pick by any reasonable rule. Do not build a matrix.
5. High impact, easy to compare: a big choice. Hand it to a decision matrix.
6. High impact, hard to compare: O'Haire's hard choice. Then apply Ruth Chang. If one option is not better, not worse, and not equal, the options are on a par. A weighted score invents a comparison. Commitment creates a reason. It does not uncover a hidden winner.

**Source.** Wes O'Haire, the hard-choice model: impact × comparability, with four cells (no-brainer, apples and oranges, big choice, hard choice). Worksheet: [Untools](https://untools.co/hard-choice-model/). Ruth Chang, "Hard Choices": in the hard-choice cell, the alternatives can be on a par, not better, worse, or equal. This page runs O'Haire's grid first and uses Chang only in that cell. Neither source uses a tragic third kind.
